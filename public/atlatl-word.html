Tonight's Blossom word

ATLATL

/ˈæt.læt.əl/  ·  at-LAT-uhl  ·  noun · Nahuatl: "spear-thrower"

The Definition, Translated

Atlatl — a grooved wooden board with a hook at one end, used to throw a dart: the dart's butt rests against the hook, and swinging the board lengthens the thrower's arm, so the dart flies far faster and farther than a hand-thrown spear.

Say it in its own rhythm — a stutter and a catch: at-LAT-uhl. The atlatl is not the weapon that hits the target; it's the weapon that throws the weapon. A lever, roughly two feet of carved wood, that gives the human arm one extra joint it was never issued. Tonight it came off a Blossom board whose pangram was mantled — atlatl is built from nothing but those letters' kin: A, T, L, and the board's own set.

From Nahuatl ahtlatl, "spear-thrower" — the Aztec name became the world's word for the whole family of throwing boards.

Anatomy of the Word

A doubled syllable and a sound English has to cheat at — tap each:

ahtlatl
The original. — In Classical Nahuatl the word is ahtlatl. Spanish scribes wrote down what they heard, English borrowed the spelling, and the word travelled further than most of the darts it threw.
-tl
One sound, two letters. — The ending tl is a single Nahuatl consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth and a puff of air out the side. English mouths can't hold it, so we sneak in a vowel and say "-tuhl" — which is why a six-letter word takes three syllables.
at · latl
The word throws twice. — Read it aloud: the same beat lands twice, at-lat, like a throw and its echo. It belongs with this collection's other doubled words — khuskhus, tangantangan — words built out of repetition.

A word that stutters on purpose, for a stick that swings twice as long as an arm.

What It Is

Three facts that have to be held together:

I. The Lever

The board is an extra joint. Arm plus atlatl swings through a longer arc than arm alone, and the dart leaves the hook with the speed that longer arc buys. Simple machine, Stone Age patent.

II. The Dart

An atlatl throws darts, not spears — long, light, feathered shafts that flex as they leave. The thrower stays in the hand; only the dart goes. You reload the stick, not the throw.

III. The Age

Spear-throwers are among humanity's oldest weapons — known across Europe, Asia, Australia (the woomera) and the Americas for tens of thousands of years, long before the bow reached most of them.

A Short History of a Long Arm

Deep Past — Everyone Invents the Lever

Carved spear-throwers survive from Ice Age Europe, some decorated with mammoths and reindeer. Aboriginal Australia kept its own version, the woomera. Wherever people hunted big animals with light arms, somebody eventually had the idea: make the arm longer.

The Aztecs — Ahtlatl

In the Aztec world the ahtlatl was both war weapon and hunting tool — soldiers carried it into battle, and hunters used it to strike waterfowl on the lakes around Tenochtitlan. The finest were carved, gilded, and carried as marks of rank.

1519 — The Conquistadors Notice

Spanish chroniclers recorded the atlatl with respect: in practiced hands its darts were reported to strike hard enough to trouble armor. Gunpowder won the century, but the throwing board had held its own against steel.

Today — A Living Sport

The atlatl never quite died. Enthusiasts organised around the World Atlatl Association throw for distance and accuracy, hunters in some places may legally take game with one, and a carved stick from the Ice Age still out-throws the bare human arm.

A lever with a memory: the arm, lengthened by two feet — and twenty thousand years. — the atlatl, in one line
