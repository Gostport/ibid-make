Tonight's Blossom word

EOAN

/iˈoʊən/  ·  ee-OH-un  ·  adjective · archaic, poetic: "of the dawn; of the east"

The Definition, Translated

Eoan — of or relating to the dawn; of or relating to the east.

Merriam-Webster's whole entry fits in one breath, because the word does exactly one job: it is the adjective for daybreak, kept by poets. It descends from Greek ēōs, dawn — from a root so old it underlies half the languages of Europe and India: Proto-Indo-European *h₂ews-, "dawn; east," because in the old world those were the same idea, the direction the light comes from. Down one road it gives Latin Aurora; down the Germanic road, Easter — Bede's dawn goddess Ēostre, feast and direction both named for the sunrise. And the word itself is a small sunrise: three vowels in a row — E, O, A — and one consonant, n, to land on.

Say it right: ee-OH-un — stress the middle and let the vowels run together like first light. Its synonyms are nearly as retired as it is: auroral, aurorean, and dilucular ("of daybreak," from Latin dīlūculum).

"But when the early-born, rosy-fingered Dawn appeared…" — Homer, the Odyssey (trans. Lattimore) — the formula that starts the day, again and again, all through the epics

What It Is

One small word, two senses, and a long scientific afterlife:

I. The Dawn Sense

Relating to the dawn: the first light, the daybreak sky, the hour before the sun clears the hill. The sense the poets kept it alive for.

II. The East Sense

Relating to the east; eastern. In the root word, dawn and east are a single thought: the quarter of the sky where the light is born.

III. The eo- Legacy

Science took the root as a prefix, eo-, "dawn of, earliest": the Eocene, dawn of the recent age; Eohippus, the dawn horse; Eoraptor, the dawn thief. Every eo- word is a sunrise.

A Short History of First Light

Rosy-Fingered — The Formula That Starts the Day

Homer's dawn arrives on schedule, in the same words, dozens of times across the Iliad and the Odyssey: rhododaktylos Ēōs, Dawn with fingers of rose-colored light. The day cannot start until she shows up — and in tonight's render, she does, literally.

The Goddess — Eos at the Gates

Personified, ēōs becomes Eos: sister of Helios the sun and Selene the moon, rising each morning from the stream of Ocean at the edge of the world to throw open heaven's gates. Rome renamed her Aurora — same root, same job.

The Poets' Word — Archaic on Arrival

Latin ēōus plus the suffix -an gives English eoan, and the dictionaries tag it "archaic, poetic" almost from birth — a word kept for when plain dawn isn't old enough. It never had a workday life; it was always Sunday dress.

Tonight — Three Vowels in a Row

On tonight's Blossom board it sat at four letters — E-O-A-N — three vowels queued up like light coming over a hill, and one n to set them down. Short and sweet: a whole sunrise in a pocket-sized word.
