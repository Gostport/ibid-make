GUIGNOL

/ɡiːnˈjɔːl/  ·  geen-YOL  ·  noun

The Definition

Guignol — the Lyonnaise puppet hero, a quick-witted silk weaver of the working class. Grand Guignol — the Paris theatre (1897–1962) famous for naturalistic horror; by extension, any gruesome, melodramatic horror entertainment.

A puppet's name became the word for an entire genre of staged gore. Show business has never been subtle.

Anatomy of the Word

Tap each fragment:

guignol
From "C'est guignolant!" — Lyon weavers' dialect for "It's funny!" The laugh gave the puppet his name: Guignol, the glove puppet in the brown wool coat with gold buttons, created by silk weaver Laurent Mourguet around 1808.
grand + guignol
French grand, "great" — the Théâtre du Grand-Guignol, "theatre of the great puppet," opened in Paris in 1897 in a former chapel. The puppet's name, scaled up to 293 seats of staged horror.

So: a weaver's joke → a puppet → a theatre → a whole genre of horror.

A Horror in Four Acts

c. 1808 — The puppet is born

Laurent Mourguet, a Lyon silk weaver turned tooth-puller turned puppeteer, creates Guignol — a working-class hero with a stick, a quick wit, and a sense of justice. He dresses him as a silk weaver, the pride of Lyon. The puppet is a hit; Polichinelle is retired.

1897 — The theatre opens

Oscar Méténier founds the Théâtre du Grand-Guignol in Paris's Pigalle district — a former chapel with 293 seats, the smallest theatre in Paris, its boxes like confessionals and angels over the orchestra. Director Max Maurey steers it toward horror and judges a show's success by the number of patrons who faint: about two a night.

1914–1945 — The golden age of gore

Between the wars, the Grand Guignol peaks — royalty and celebrities in evening dress come to watch eye-gougings, live burials, and acid attacks staged with pioneering special effects. André de Lorde, the "Prince of Terror," writes over a hundred plays, consulting a psychologist on the madness scenes. Doctors sit in the audience as a marketing ploy.

1962 → — The afterlife

The theatre closes — real postwar horror, management said, had outpaced the stage. But the name survives as an adjective: grand-guignolesque, for anything theatrically gruesome. Every splatter film since owes the puppet royalties.

The Puppet That Ate Paris

The Grand Guignol's genius was intimacy: 293 seats, a converted chapel, effects close enough to touch. The horror wasn't spectacle — it was proximity. Audiences fainted not because the gore was grand, but because it was near.

"The theatre owed its name to Guignol, a traditional Lyonnaise puppet character, joining political commentary with the style of Punch and Judy." — on the Théâtre du Grand-Guignol (1897–1962)
